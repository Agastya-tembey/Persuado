Bloc Building

Bloc building is one of the most important skills in Model United Nations. It is the process of identifying which countries are likely to support your position, which countries are likely to oppose it, and which countries may be persuaded to work with you. Strong bloc building allows delegates to gain co-sponsors, gather votes, negotiate amendments, and ultimately increase the chances of their resolution being passed.

To effectively answer questions related to bloc building, a delegate should first conduct thorough research on every country represented in the committee. This research should include each country's foreign policy priorities, political alliances, voting patterns in the United Nations, regional affiliations, economic interests, military partnerships, and historical relationships with other member states. Understanding these factors helps delegates predict how countries may react to different proposals and identify potential partners for cooperation.

One of the most effective methods for organizing this information is to create a detailed Excel spreadsheet containing all countries represented in the committee. The spreadsheet should include the name of every delegation along with relevant research categories such as:

  • Political allies
  • Political rivals or adversaries
  • Regional organizations
  • Economic partnerships
  • Major policy positions
  • Voting tendencies
  • Key national interests
  • Potential areas of cooperation

A simple colour-coding system can then be used to quickly visualize relationships:

🟢 Green – Countries that are likely allies, supporters, or natural partners.
🔴 Red – Countries that are likely opponents, competitors, or states with conflicting interests.
🔵 Blue – Countries that are neutral, undecided, or whose position is unclear and may be influenced through negotiation.

Additional columns can be added to record important notes, potential negotiation points, voting predictions, and whether a delegation is likely to become a sponsor or signatory of a draft resolution. This allows delegates to quickly assess the committee landscape, prioritize diplomatic outreach, and keep track of changing positions throughout the conference.

Beyond research, delegates should actively engage in diplomatic outreach. This includes speaking with other delegates during unmoderated caucuses, identifying shared priorities, proposing compromises, and finding areas of mutual benefit. Successful bloc building often depends not only on policy alignment but also on communication skills, persuasion, and relationship-building.

When analyzing bloc dynamics, delegates should ask themselves several key questions:

  • Which countries have interests most similar to my delegation?
  • Which countries consistently vote together in real-world international organizations?
  • Which regional groups are likely to support or oppose my proposals?
  • What concessions could encourage neutral countries to join my bloc?
  • Which countries possess significant influence and could attract additional supporters?
  • How can I expand my bloc without compromising my country's core policies?

A well-organized Excel spreadsheet combined with comprehensive country research, strategic networking, and active negotiation provides a systematic framework for understanding committee dynamics. By identifying allies, opponents, and neutral delegations through clear visual indicators such as green, red, and blue colour coding, delegates can make informed decisions, build stronger coalitions, secure sponsorship for draft resolutions, and maximize their influence throughout the committee. Ultimately, effective bloc building is not simply about identifying friends and opponents; it is about understanding the broader diplomatic landscape and strategically assembling a coalition capable of advancing your country's objectives and securing support for its proposals.
