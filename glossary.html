General MUN Terminology

Learn the foundational terms that describe the people, process, and tools used in every committee room.

9 entries
General MUN Terminology

Model United Nations

Definition: A simulation of United Nations committees where students act as delegates representing countries to discuss global issues, draft resolutions, and practice diplomacy.

Practical example: In a MUN conference, a delegate for Brazil may propose a climate action plan while collaborating with other states on amendments.

Common mistakes: Assuming it is the same as a debate club; MUN emphasizes negotiation, policy solutions, and formal procedure rather than adversarial argument alone.

Delegate

Definition: A student participant who represents a country, NGO, or bloc in committee, presenting positions and negotiating resolutions.

Practical example: As delegate for Kenya, you explain your country’s priorities on food security and vote on amendments accordingly.

Common mistakes: Confusing delegate responsibilities with personal opinion; delegates should advocate their assigned position, not their own preferences.

Chair

Definition: The student leader who manages debate, enforces rules of procedure, and guides the committee through motions, speaker lists, and voting.

Practical example: The chair calls the committee to order, asks for motions, and tracks time during speeches.

Common mistakes: Ignoring the chair’s instructions; the chair controls procedure, and delegates must request recognition before speaking.

Secretariat

Definition: The administrative team responsible for organizing the conference, preparing materials, and supporting chairs and delegates.

Practical example: The secretariat circulates the agenda, collects position papers, and publishes committee updates.

Common mistakes: Thinking the secretariat is part of committee debate; they remain impartial and focus on event logistics.

Committee

Definition: A group of delegates simulating a UN body or crisis team that debates a specific agenda item.

Practical example: The Security Council committee discusses sanctions, while the WHO committee debates global health policy.

Common mistakes: Treating committees as informal discussion groups; committees follow strict rules and formal document procedures.

Agenda

Definition: The official topic or list of issues the committee will address during the conference.

Practical example: “Improving Access to Clean Water” is the agenda topic for a special session of the Economic and Social Council.

Common mistakes: Trying to discuss unrelated issues; delegates must keep proposals aligned with the agenda item that has been set.

Quorum

Definition: The minimum number of delegates required for the committee to conduct official business and vote on motions.

Practical example: If a committee has 50 members and the rules specify quorum as a simple majority, at least 26 delegates must be present.

Common mistakes: Beginning formal votes without quorum; decisions made without the required attendance are invalid.

Placard

Definition: A card bearing a delegate’s country name used to request the floor, vote, or show position during committee sessions.

Practical example: Raise your placard to speak, to submit a motion, or to request a roll call vote.

Common mistakes: Forgetting to keep the placard visible; chairs and fellow delegates rely on it to recognize speakers and count votes.

Debate & Speaking

6 entries
Debate & Speaking

General Speakers List

Definition: The ordered list of delegates who will speak during formal debate, managed by the chair and often used at the start of the session.

Practical example: Delegates sign up for the speakers list, and the chair calls them one by one for 60-second statements.

Common mistakes: Speaking out of turn or not listening to the chair; the speakers list is the official queue for speeches.

Yield

Definition: When a delegate finishes their speech and gives remaining speaking time to the chair, another delegate, or the floor for questions.

Practical example: A delegate yields time to questions after delivering a position statement on humanitarian aid.

Common mistakes: Yielding without specifying the destination; the chair needs clear direction to manage the next speaker.

Yield to Chair

Definition: A yield option that returns any unused time back to the chair instead of another delegate or to questions.

Practical example: A delegate concludes early and yields remaining time to the chair so the committee can proceed.

Common mistakes: Believing this gives another delegate extra time; it simply returns the time to committee control.

Yield to Questions

Definition: A yield option that allows other delegates to ask brief questions after a speech.

Practical example: After a speech on climate policy, the delegate yields to questions and answers inquiries from allies.

Common mistakes: Treating it as debate time rather than as a chance for short clarifications.

Yield to Another Delegate

Definition: A yield option where the remaining speaking time is given to a specific delegate instead of the chair or floor.

Practical example: A delegate yields to another delegate to support a joint point or follow-up statement.

Common mistakes: Failing to name the delegate clearly; the chair must know who will speak next.

Right of Reply

Definition: A special procedural privilege allowing a delegate to respond to a personal insult or direct attack after permission is granted by the chair.

Practical example: When another delegate criticizes your country’s policy, you may request the right of reply to answer formally.

Common mistakes: Using it for normal rebuttal; it is reserved for personal statements that directly affect the delegate or country.

Motions

Motions

Motion

Definition: A formal proposal made by a delegate to change the flow of committee business, such as opening debate or changing the speakers list.

Practical example: A delegate motions for a moderated caucus to focus discussion on financing.

Common mistakes: Believing motions are informal suggestions; motions must be seconded and voted on before taking effect.

Moderated Caucus

Definition: A structured informal debate period where delegates speak in short turns on a specific sub-topic under the chair’s direction.

When to use it: When the committee needs focused discussion on a single part of the agenda with time-limited contributions.

Why it matters: It allows delegates to clarify positions, negotiate language, and build momentum toward resolution drafting.

Example scenario: Delegates vote for a 10-minute moderated caucus on “funding mechanisms for humanitarian aid.”

Common mistakes: Treating it like general debate; delegates must speak through the chair and respect time limits.

Unmoderated Caucus

Definition: A block of time during which delegates leave their seats to consult informally, build blocs, and draft resolutions without the chair calling on speakers.

When to use it: When delegates need to negotiate directly, exchange ideas, or prepare working papers.

Why it matters: It creates space for collaboration and coalition-building that often determines the success of a draft resolution.

Example scenario: Delegates request a 15-minute unmoderated caucus to finalize sponsor language.

Common mistakes: Failing to stay on the agenda topic or allowing one group to monopolize the discussion.

Motion to Set the Agenda

Definition: A procedural motion to determine which topic or issue the committee will discuss first.

Practical example: After opening statements, delegates vote on whether to address “cybersecurity” before “global migration.”

Common mistakes: Offering it after substantive debate has started; agenda decisions occur early in committee.

Motion to Suspend Debate

Definition: A motion to pause formal debate temporarily, often to allow an unmoderated caucus or a break.

Practical example: Delegates motion to suspend debate for 10 minutes so they can work on a draft resolution.

Common mistakes: Confusing it with adjournment; suspension is temporary and the committee will resume later.

Motion to Close Debate

Definition: A motion to end debate on the current topic and move to voting on the draft resolution or amendment.

Practical example: A delegate who believes the committee has finished discussing a draft resolution may motion to close debate.

Common mistakes: Making it too early; chairs often require a majority and ensure delegates have had adequate time to speak.

Motion to Adjourn the Meeting

Definition: A motion to end the committee session entirely, closing the meeting until the next scheduled time.

Practical example: At the end of the day, the committee votes to adjourn until tomorrow.

Common mistakes: Attempting to use it to pause debate temporarily; it ends the entire meeting instead.

Motion to Adjourn Debate

Definition: A motion to end discussion on one resolution or topic while keeping the committee open for other business.

Practical example: Delegates may motion to adjourn debate on a unpopular draft resolution so the committee can consider another proposal.

Common mistakes: Assuming it ends the whole session; it only closes debate on the current matter.

Motion to Reconsider

Definition: A motion to reopen debate or reconsider a decision that has already been made, typically requiring special rules or a seconded sponsor.

Practical example: If a resolution fails but new information emerges, a delegate may motion to reconsider the vote.

Common mistakes: Using it to reverse routine decisions; it is reserved for decisions that merit a formal second review.

Points

Points are short procedural requests used to seek clarification, correct the record, or address personal comfort during debate.

4 entries
Points

Point of Information

Definition: A request for clarification directed at the chair or a speaking delegate, typically used after a speech or during debate.

When to use it: When you need a fact verified, want more detail on a statement, or seek clarification on procedure.

Why it matters: It keeps debate precise and ensures delegates are answering the right questions.

Example scenario: After a speech on sanctions, you say, “Point of information to the speaker: what timeline is your country proposing?”

Common mistakes: Using it as a speech instead of a brief question; it is meant for quick clarification, not extended argument.

Point of Personal Privilege

Definition: A procedural point used when a delegate is unable to participate due to personal discomfort, such as poor audibility or temperature.

Practical example: “Point of personal privilege: I cannot hear the speaker clearly.”

Common mistakes: Using it to interrupt debate for unrelated complaints; it is specifically for physical or audibility issues.

Point of Parliamentary Inquiry

Definition: A question directed to the chair about procedure, timing, or how to raise a motion.

Practical example: “Point of parliamentary inquiry: may I yield to the delegate for questions after my speech?”

Common mistakes: Asking substantive policy questions; it should only address procedure or the rules of order.

Point of Order

Definition: A correction raised when a delegate believes the rules of procedure are being violated.

When to use it: When committee procedure has been broken, such as speaking out of turn or incorrect vote counting.

Why it matters: It preserves fair process and ensures that decisions are legitimate.

Example scenario: If a delegate speaks without recognition, another delegate may say, “Point of order: the speaker is out of turn.”

Common mistakes: Using it to challenge a substance or tone; it is strictly procedural, not argumentative.

Resolutions & Amendments

Working Paper

Definition: An informal draft document shared among delegates to begin collaboration before a draft resolution is formally submitted.

Practical example: Delegates circulate a working paper outlining proposed preambulatory and operative clauses to verify support.

Common mistakes: Treating it as a final text; working papers are still flexible and may change significantly.

Draft Resolution

Definition: A formal proposed resolution submitted for debate and voting, containing preambulatory and operative clauses.

When to use it: When a group has agreed on a policy solution and is ready to move to formal committee consideration.

Why it matters: It becomes the basis for structured debate and can be amended or voted on by the committee.

Example scenario: After several informal sessions, a coalition submits a draft resolution on refugee protection.

Common mistakes: Waiting too long to submit or submitting a draft without enough sponsors; that reduces its chances of being heard.

See also: sample resolution

Resolution

Definition: A document adopted by the committee that expresses the collective decision on how to address the agenda issue.

When to use it: When the committee has reached a consensus or voting majority for a proposal.

Why it matters: It is the final official record of the committee’s agreed policy recommendations.

Example scenario: The committee passes a resolution recommending measures to improve global education access.

Common mistakes: Confusing a draft resolution with an adopted resolution; only after a successful vote does a draft become an official resolution.

See also: sample resolution

Sponsor

Definition: A delegate or group of delegates who formally author and support a draft resolution.

Practical example: Three countries sign as sponsors on a draft resolution proposing climate aid.

Common mistakes: Listing too few sponsors; many committees require a minimum number for submission.

See also: sample resolution

Signatory

Definition: A delegate who supports a draft resolution enough to endorse its submission but is not a primary author.

Practical example: Several delegates sign as signatories to show they will consider the draft resolution for discussion.

Common mistakes: Believing signatories automatically vote yes; they only guarantee the draft will be debated.

See also: sample resolution

Friendly Amendment

Definition: A change to a draft resolution agreed to by all sponsors and introduced without a formal vote.

Practical example: Sponsors revise wording to make a clause more inclusive, and the chair accepts it as friendly.

Common mistakes: Assuming all delegates can approve it; only sponsors need to agree for a friendly amendment.

Unfriendly Amendment

Definition: An amendment to a draft resolution that is not approved by all sponsors and must be voted on separately.

Practical example: A delegate proposes an unfriendly amendment to strengthen sanctions language.

Common mistakes: Believing that an unfriendly amendment can be added without debate; it requires a vote and may fail.

Preambulatory Clause

Definition: The introductory phrases in a draft resolution that explain the context, concerns, and legal basis for the proposed action.

Practical example: “Recognizing the urgent need for sustainable water management…” is a preambulatory clause.

Common mistakes: Using operative language in preambulatory clauses; they should not contain commands.

See also: sample resolution

Operative Clause

Definition: The action-oriented clauses in a draft resolution that explain what the committee recommends or decides.

Practical example: “Calls upon member states to provide funding for renewable energy projects…” is an operative clause.

Common mistakes: Writing vague or overly broad operative clauses; they should be specific and actionable.

See also: sample resolution

Voting Procedures

Simple Majority

Definition: A vote in which more than half of the delegates voting must vote in favor for a motion or resolution to pass.

Practical example: If 40 delegates vote yes or no, at least 21 yes votes are needed.

Common mistakes: Counting abstentions as yes; they are excluded from the yes/no total.

Two-Thirds Majority

Definition: A vote requiring two-thirds of delegates voting to approve, often used for important procedural motions or amendments.

Practical example: For 30 votes cast, at least 20 must be yes.

Common mistakes: Forgetting that abstentions reduce the denominator only if they count as votes not cast in some committees; always confirm the rules.

Roll Call Vote

Definition: A vote where the chair calls each country’s name and records their individual vote aloud.

Practical example: The chair reads each country and each delegate responds yes, no, or abstain.

Common mistakes: Speaking before your country is called; the chair controls the order.

Division of the Question

Definition: A motion to split a draft resolution into separate parts so delegates can vote on them independently.

Practical example: A delegate motions to divide a resolution so the committee votes on the funding clause separately from the enforcement clause.

Common mistakes: Asking to divide too broadly; the chair may deny if it would make voting confusing.

Abstention

Definition: A decision by a delegate to neither support nor oppose a vote.

Practical example: If a delegate does not fully agree with a resolution but does not wish to block it, they may abstain.

Common mistakes: Using it as a protest vote; abstaining is a neutral option, not a statement of opposition.

Veto Power

Definition: The ability of certain powerful members, such as permanent members of the Security Council, to block a resolution by voting no.

Practical example: If a permanent member says no on a Security Council resolution, the resolution fails despite majority support.

Common mistakes: Believing it applies in all committees; only specific bodies like the Security Council grant veto power.

Documents

Position Paper

Definition: A concise document that outlines a delegate’s country position, priorities, and proposed solutions on the agenda topic.

Practical example: Delegates submit position papers before the conference to demonstrate research and policy ideas.

Common mistakes: Turning it into an essay; it should be clear, organized, and directly tied to the agenda.

Resolution

Definition: The final document a committee approves to address the agenda issue, including both preambulatory and operative clauses.

Practical example: After debate and amendments, a resolution is adopted to coordinate international action on climate change.

Common mistakes: Confusing the draft stage with the final adopted resolution; only adopted texts become official committee positions.

Amendment

Definition: A proposed change to a draft resolution that modifies, adds, or removes specific wording.

Practical example: Delegates propose an amendment to strengthen the wording of an operative clause on aid distribution.

Common mistakes: Proposing amendments without sponsor consent; they often require formal submission and a vote.

Communiqué

Definition: A brief statement issued by a committee or crisis body to explain its decisions or position to the broader conference.

Practical example: After a rapid response session, delegates release a communiqué summarizing their agreed course of action.

Common mistakes: Using it as a substitute for a resolution; it is a statement, not a formal policy document.

Directive

Definition: A command or instruction issued in crisis committees to move the narrative or assign actions to delegates.

Practical example: In a crisis simulation, the chair issues a directive asking a delegate to coordinate evacuation efforts.

Common mistakes: Treating directives like resolutions; they are usually committee or crisis-specific orders, not full policy documents.

Understand the different roles and groups that shape alliances, information flow, and representation in committee rooms.

5 entries
Committees & Delegates

Permanent Member

Definition: In some UN bodies, a state with ongoing membership and special procedural privileges, such as veto power in the Security Council.

Practical example: The United States, China, Russia, France, and the United Kingdom are permanent members of the Security Council.

Common mistakes: Assuming all committees have permanent members; only certain UN organs use this concept.

Observer State

Definition: A state or organization allowed to attend committee sessions without full voting rights.

Practical example: The Holy See or Palestine may observe Security Council debate but cannot vote on resolutions.

Common mistakes: Treating them as full members; observers typically cannot sponsor or vote on resolutions.

NGO

Definition: A non-governmental organization that may participate in certain MUN simulations to represent civil society perspectives.

Practical example: An NGO delegate may advocate for human rights or environmental protection in a committee.

Common mistakes: Expecting NGO delegates to act as countries; they represent an organization’s interest, not a national position.

Bloc

Definition: A group of delegates with similar interests who work together to support resolutions and motions.

Practical example: Delegates from African nations may form an African bloc to coordinate on development issues.

Common mistakes: Forming a bloc too rigidly; successful blocs remain open to negotiation and compromise.

Coalition

Definition: A temporary partnership of delegates or blocs working together on a specific draft resolution or motion.

Practical example: A coalition of small states and environmental advocates drafts an amendment to strengthen climate commitments.

Common mistakes: Assuming a coalition is permanent; it often dissolves after the issue is resolved.

Diplomacy & Negotiation

Learn the soft skills that help delegates build trust, reach agreement, and represent their country’s interests effectively.

4 entries
Diplomacy & Negotiation

Bloc Building

Definition: The process of forming alliances with other delegates who share similar goals or policy priorities.

Practical example: You may work with delegates from Latin American countries to support a common development proposal.

Common mistakes: Rushing to form a bloc without understanding others’ positions; effective bloc building requires listening and trust.

Negotiation

Definition: The give-and-take process of finding common ground, adjusting positions, and creating workable solutions.

Practical example: Negotiating the wording of a clause so that both developed and developing countries feel represented.

Common mistakes: Treating it as winning or losing; good negotiation seeks mutually acceptable outcomes.

Consensus

Definition: General agreement among delegates where objections are minimal and the body can adopt a proposal without contentious debate.

Practical example: The committee reaches consensus on a broadly supported recommendation to improve education access.

Common mistakes: Assuming consensus means unanimous agreement; it means no strong opposition remains.

Diplomacy

Definition: The art of managing international relations through tact, respect, and persuasive communication.

Practical example: A delegate phrases criticism carefully and offers constructive alternatives to keep negotiations moving.

Common mistakes: Being overly aggressive or confrontational; diplomacy depends on maintaining professional tone and relationships.

Advanced Parliamentary Procedure

Advanced Parliamentary Procedure

Suspension of Rules

Definition: A motion to temporarily suspend specific rules in order to allow a different procedure or to expedite committee business.

Practical example: Delegates motion to suspend the speakers list to allow an immediate unmoderated caucus.

Common mistakes: Assuming all rules can be suspended; only the specific rules stated in the motion can be paused.

Closure of Debate

Definition: Another term for a motion to close debate on the current draft resolution or amendment.

Practical example: When debate has been thorough, delegates may make a motion to close debate and move to voting.

Common mistakes: Pushing it too early; chairs often request sufficient speaking opportunities before closing debate.

Tabling

Definition: A motion to set a draft resolution or amendment aside temporarily, often to consider other documents first.

Practical example: Delegates table a controversial draft resolution to focus on a stronger proposal.

Common mistakes: Confusing tabling with adjournment; tabling pauses consideration, but the committee remains open.

Reconsideration

Definition: A procedural motion to reopen debate on a decision or vote that has recently taken place.

Practical example: A delegate motions to reconsider a resolution after new evidence is presented.

Common mistakes: Assuming it is a regular motion; it often requires special rules and may be limited to certain timeframes.

Parliamentary Procedure

Definition: The rules and customs that govern how committees conduct business, including motions, voting, and speaking rights.

Practical example: Following parliamentary procedure ensures motions are made, seconded, and voted on in an orderly manner.

Common mistakes: Ignoring procedure when you disagree; proper procedure protects fairness and gives every delegate a chance to participate.
